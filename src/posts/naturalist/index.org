** Of machines, mushrooms and marvel

As usual, the rambl-y preliminary part first, and this time I have no intention to keep it short (I never do, but usually I intend to). You can, however, @@html:<a href="#concept"> skip this </a>@@

Let's face it, I have a soft spot for the odd and the weird. The sort of things that make you look twice, the perversely overengineered ones as well as the stupidly simple genius ones (you know what I'm talking about, it's a genre). I love building them myself, and it makes all my circuits light up to awe upon what other people come up with. And I think a lot of the most exciting things live between two worlds and I, too, try to happily merge things where possible (more for practical reasons, but hey, let me have my little philosophical escapade here).

So, prepare for a little LJMP: for this particular project I merged two of my lifelong obsessions, machines and mushrooms.

I really love mushrooms. It brings me joy to look at them, to boop them. I usually don't lick them. And not just mushrooms, the entire realm of fungi is stimulatingly systematic, there's always a new rabbit hole to dive into headfirst, always one more thing to discover.

And then there's machines (obviously). Automatons, apparatuses, computers, contraptions, instruments. I'm not particularly picky about them: I collect a lot of things, from ThinkPads to retro gaming consoles to antique measuring tools.

So, psychologically speaking, it was probably inevitable that at some point I'd combine these interests into a tiny computer stuffed into an antique pencil case.
Enter the Naturalist, my personal Pilzapparat (= mushroom apparatus). It accompanies me on my walks, helps me evaluate mushrooms, lets me virtually collect them without removing anything from the forest, and turns their historical illustrations into little pieces of art.

And there's this hard-to-describe warm feeling that someone 150 years ago meticulously painted a mushroom illustration, possibly as in awe as I am when I look at one, and today, in 2026, some random weird woman (hi) gets to enjoy their work.

I think that's what I cherish most about oddly specific creations. They're little windows into what someone else finds fascinating, a little lens I can look through to see how another being perceives the world around them (that sentence sounded cute in my head, but kinda creepy written down). And I don't even have to share their particular passion to enjoy their enthusiasm. Sometimes I learn something new, sometimes I stumble into an entirely unexpected rabbit hole, and sometimes I even make a friend along the way.

Well, I suppose that's what I attempt to do here: opening one of these windows from the other side. Maybe some will enjoy the view, maybe it'll push them down a rabbit hole of their own. And if someone else happens to tune into this specific wavelength of weirdness and sees a bit of their own weirdness reflected back, that'd be nice, too.

In this spirit, welcome to my workshop, there be mushrooms (and yeah, also the actual project, hidden under a paragraph of pseudo-philosophical escapades).

** Move slow and look at things: the Naturalist's concept

The Naturalist is deliberately built around slowness. And while this sounds like a horrible marketing slogan trying to sell you yoga accessories you don't need, it actually sums up my goal quite well.

I enjoy taking my time with things, exploring details, taking a second look. So for a device that is supposed to accompany me on my lengthy walks, taking things slow is a core requirement.

I feel this particular cyberdeck of mine really lives up to this requirement. And this starts with the process of building it:

Back in March when I acquired a new old ammeter, I had the random idea of building a device that resembles the measuring instruments I collect. And, well, I could just have acquired any wooden case, or even built one myself. But for both aesthetic reasons and the satisfaction of finding the right one™, it's much more fun to search for the right one™. So, from March onward, I had my little antennas stretched out to find a case that just hit the spot. And in August, I finally did (as well as some nice donor loot for it).

Designing the keyboard PCB was equally slow: I wanted the whole thing to resemble antique measuring tools, or something a 19th-century naturalist would carry around as a field instrument. So I spent a lot of time turning botanical and zoological illustrations into ENIG artwork.

Okay, mutilating that wooden case with a Dremel did not take that much time. However, fitting it back together so it didn't look too mutilated anymore did.

The only "deadline pressure" I faced here was caused by the weather. I had to temporarily interrupt my [[https://missmolerat.com/posts/clotty_intro/][Clotty]] work to get some test mushroom walks in before the temperatures became mushroom-hostile.

But most importantly, the slowness isn't limited to the process of building that contraption: the only way to use it is slowly.

And yeah, the eInk I'm using, with its refresh time of ~30s (depending on the temperature), certainly helps enforce this. But the whole user interface is an invitation to explore and observe, too. There's no automated mushroom identification, image classification or AI that identifies mushrooms for you. After all, the machine is built to bring me joy, and not to take away what brings me joy.

I want to marvel at mushrooms. I want to inspect them and poke them and do weird stretching to see what's under the cap. So all I do is enter shortcuts from a defined trait vocabulary for the mushroom characteristics I've observed. And the Naturalist then presents me with scored search results from my mushroom database and their corresponding historical illustrations. I choose which mushroom is likely the one in front of me, put it in my virtual basket and carry my finds home to display them as art collages on my TV.

The illustrations are not random either: they're hand-selected historical illustrations from the [[https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/][Biodiversity Heritage Library]] (and oh! Did I find some wonderful rabbit holes to go down in the process, see @@html:<a href="#data"> the data section </a>@@). Meaning, 150 years ago, someone else walked through a forest or over a meadow, inspected a mushroom, maybe booped it, and then turned it into the wonderful illustration that now lives on a device they couldn't even imagine back then (yes, yes, I know that's not necessarily how it was done, but let me have this fantasy, okay?). A little mycelium-network-link through time.

By now, I've had the chance to take the Naturalist on a few walks and can happily confirm that it does indeed slow me down. Since I don't have to take out my phone for a pic but have to really focus on the specimen in front of me to identify its traits, everything else narrows down. Until there's nothing else, just mushroom.

Which, to someone like me who has the mobility of an armchair, feels a lot more relaxing than yoga anyways.

** From pencil case to Pilzapparat

For technical details, keyboard BOM and so on, please see [[https://github.com/bitshiftcrazy/naturalist/tree/main/hardware][GitHub]], this section here is more of a build gallery.

To summarise the main parts quickly, the hardware of this deck is unsurprisingly simple: a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W, a Pimoroni Inky Impression Spectra 6, a custom RP2040-based keyboard PCB, an Adafruit PowerBoost 1000C, some sprinkles like LEDs, toggle switches etc.

While the resulting device is kinda cute (in the right light), the process, in tradition of how I usually build (butcher) things like this, was not.

The keyboard has 42 push buttons and visible diodes so it looks a bit more like an instrument. Generally, quite a few electronics remain visible on the Naturalist to make it feel less "polished mini computer" and more "could pass as an instrument".
Don't ask me why I went for the weird keyboard layout, tho, I thought it'd kinda look cool and split-keyboard-y, but... I guess two more keys would not have changed the look significantly.

It also has a small silkscreen area for handwritten notes with wet-erasable markers. I wasn't sure whether this would work well or if it'd leave stains, so far I've only used silkscreen with permanent marker. But it turns out to work precisely as I intended.
And as mentioned before, the front ENIG artwork is based on illustrations from the Biodiversity Heritage Library as well. Big shoutout to KiCad's "simplify polygons" feature here. I suck at artwork and usually create the ones for my PCBs in inkscape (with which I suck even more), and that's totally feasible for small shapes. But for botanical paintings at my skill level, well, not so much. The only reason I did not hire a professional artist for my obsucre hobby project was that I could not find one...

** Bits, buttons and boletes

For software details...yes, [[https://github.com/bitshiftcrazy/naturalist/tree/main/software][GitHub]] again.

The Naturalist runs on a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W, which is a ridiculous amount of computing power for what it does, but that decision was based on "works well with that display I have here".

The software itself is written in C, with Cairo and Pango doing the rendering and Jansson for JSON, for an equally well-founded reason: since I earn the money for my expensive hobbies by writing C, it's kinda the only language I have proficiency in (haven't really touched anything else since uni). There is a small Python bridge for the Inky display, though. And keyboard input is handled through evdev.

You could probably write the whole thing in Python or whatever suits you more, there's really no "hard technical reason" to use C.

The interface itself is designed around the long refresh period of a colour eInk: you simply want as few refreshes as possible, so no scrolling or endless menu navigation. And that's very much in the spirit of the deck anyways!

** Of fungi, facets, and falsely attributed Frenchmen

/this is the most exciting part ;)/

As promised above, the curation of both my mushroom data and the historical illustrations led me down quite a few exciting rabbit holes!

For now, I have 25 of the most common mushroom species in my little database. And given that I curate the whole thing by myself (hälp), that was enough work for a start.

As mentioned before, identification is not automated, but done by myself. So I had to collect characteristics I can actually observe and turn them into a (relatively) minimal vocabulary: cap shape, primary colour, habitat, gills, rings, stems, and so on and so forth.

It was truly satisfying to discover all the things there are to discover, the little details you usually don't pay attention to, and to figure out what makes a mushroom identifiable among the others. And funnily enough, for some, it really comes down to surprisingly few things that make them stand out! Take the snakeskin markings on the stem of a parasol mushroom, the spines underneath the cap of a hedgehog mushroom, or bruising colour. Other things that seem like obvious and immediately visible are surprisingly un-conclusive, such as cap colour (unless it's blue, I guess).

For example, I took some of the illustrations from [[https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/4063][Atlas des champignons comestibles et vénéneux]] (1891), by Léon Dufour. But while digging into the author's work, I stumbled upon [[https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/183275][Nouvelle flore des champignons]] (1891), which the Biodiversity Heritage Library attributes to Julien Costantin and Léon Dufour (1780–1865). Which is an even more remarkable achievement than the mushroom research, given that Dufour, accoriding to these dates, had been dead for 26 years when the book was published.

Now, this certainly could have had an explanation, for example, his work may simply have been published after his death. But as it turned out, there were actually two French naturalists who don't just share their interest in mushrooms, but also their name Léon Dufour. And the book was written by the younger one, [[https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb107474856][Léon Marie Dufour (1861–1942)]]. There's even this note saying "Ne pas confondre avec : Dufour, Léon (1860?-19..)" in the record for [[https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb11974260p][Léon Dufour (1780–1865)]].

Very funny, very confusing, and very hard to resist the German urge to write an email to the Biodiversity Heritage Library to ask them to correct this.
And: it's not clear who actually created the illustrations, since I couldn't find a credit for the illustrator (or perhaps the younger Dufour did them himself, who knows, I certainly don't, and I was informed it'd be slightly too much to book a trip to France to look for a lost illustrator in some archives...).

But even more pedantic to spot, and even harder to resist the urge to correct:

During my rabbit-hole crawling, I also stumbled upon [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Maria_Hussey][Anna Maria Hussey]], a British mycologist and illustrator, whose marvellous paintings found a new home in this cyberdeck, too. She published her work under the name "Mrs. T. J. Hussey", using her husband's initials, as was unfortunately customary at the time. And while the Biodiversity Heritage Library correctly lists her (and her sister Frances Reed) as an author and illustrator of [[https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/3606][Illustrations of British Mycology]], the embedded image metadata of the illustrations still attributes them to her husband, Thomas John Hussey. Apparently, even 170 years later, female researchers can end up in the shadow of their husbands in one way or another.

And just like that, trying to find pretty pictures for my mushroom computer turned into an investigation of 19th-century naturalists. Totally worth it.

Obviously all illustrations have to be Public Domain or Not in Copyright to go into the Naturalist (see GitHub for details [[https://github.com/bitshiftcrazy/naturalist/tree/main/software/data][here]]).

But there's another important consideration with historical data for the Naturalist: to keep it as asshole-free as possible.

The thing is, history is dirty, and I didn't want to end up accidentally contaminating my marvel with a Nazi mushroom illustration. So, for example, German authors/illustrators who were adults and professionally active in Nazi Germany between 1933 and 1945 are generally ruled out, unless there's significant evidence of opposition to the regime or persecution by it (for example, resistance members or Jewish artists). This doesn't mean I assume everyone who continued publishing during that period was a Nazi, but I simply don't want to investigate every ambiguous biography to clear someone for my little mushroom database. I'd rather err on the side of caution.
Other time periods and nationalities get a background check to at least check for obvious assholery. Sure, after a century, there's no way to be 100% certain, but I at least want to do my due diligence.
